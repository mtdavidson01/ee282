# EE282 Project Topic Proposal

## Introduction

Bacteriophages (phages), viruses of bacteria, are the most abundant part of the biosphere at an estimated $10^{31}$particles worldwide (Hendrix et al. 1999; Mushegian 2020). With such abundance and ubiquity, understanding phage-bacteria interactions can be useful across investigations of bacterial physiology, ecology, evolution, and pathogenesis. One useful way to gain insight into these interactions is through the selection for phage resistant mutant bacteria, which can be used to investigate both broad and system-specific biological questions. An archetypical example of this comes from Luria and Delbruck's work demonstrating that resistance to a lytic phage in a bacterial culture arises from pre-existing variation within the population, rather than being induced by exposure to the phage (Luria and Delbruck 1943). The isolation of phage resistant mutants allows for further investigation into both how resistance arises and how mutations conferring resistance may have pleiotropic consequences that impact fitness in ecologically or clinically important contexts. For example, phage A1-1 selection of _Shigella flexneri_ resulted in isolates resistant to the phage, but also deficient for intercellular spread, a key virulence trait (Kortright et al. 2022). As sequencing has become dramatically cheaper and more accessible over time, it has become common to sequence resistant isolates and identify mutations likely to confer phage resistance. These genomic insights can be integrated with existing phenotypic data and existing knowledge of host genetics to infer mechanisms by which mutations confer resistance. In addition to streamlining further experimental investigation, this genetic characterization also allows for concerted investigation of context-dependent fitness differences between classes of resistance mutations. These remain useful in investigations of how phage-bacteria interactions fit into larger ecological and evolutionary dynamics of microbial communities, as well as in developing effective and evolutionarily informed practices as phage therapy becomes more and more prominent in the global west (Alseth et al. 2019; Burmeister et al. 2020; Kortright et al. 2019).

## Body

Using genomic methods to determine phage resistance mutations is now easy and cost-effective enough that isolating and sequencing phage resistant mutants is a common first step in basic investigations of phage-bacteria interactions, and may be on its way to becoming a standard step in the characterization of novel phages. Once resistance mutations are identified, they may provide insight into biological questions of interest. For example, what pathways, structures, or cellular processes are these genes involved in, and how might they be disrupted by the mutations? How might that disruption prevent a successful phage infection cycle, and what stage of infection might no longer be viable? How might these disruptions affect bacterial fitness in a microbial community, or severity of an infection?

To probe these questions, the genomic differences between an initially susceptible ancestral strain and one or more phage resistant mutants must be identified. Phage resistant mutants are, in general, readily able to be selected for, isolated, and sequenced. These can then be compared to the ancestral genome and used to call variants, or identify genomic differences. If the ancestral strain is a commonly used lab strain, high quality reference genomes are likely publicly available. If not, the ancestral strain must first be sequenced, assembled, and annotated in order to perform variant calling and identify putative resistance mutations. The Whiteson lab, which I am currently rotating in, has a collection of bacterial clinical isolates and a library of phages. Recently, three _Stenotrophomonas maltophilia_ clinical isolates were each subjected to selection by a different infecting phage. For each phage-host combination, roughly five phage resistant mutants were isolated and sequenced alongside the ancestral bacteria. The sequence data from these samples was recently received but has yet to be analyzed, and represents an ideal dataset for the identification of putative phage resistance mutations through the process of assembly, annotation, and variant calling outlined above. Mutations identified in resistant isolates will be analyzed to determine how they disrupt phage susceptibility, and if this in turn may have pleiotropic effects on virulence or antibiotic resistance phenotypes relevant to their clinical origins.

## Conclusion

This project represents a common, straightforward, and useful way of using sequencing data to probe what mutations may underlie a favored phenotype under strong selective pressure, in this case, resistance to a phage. Using isolates derived from a bacterial culture exposed to phage selection rather than the whole population additionally increases the likelihood of producing clear, easy to interpret data through these analyses. The assembly, annotation, and variant calling required all have good, established, and commonly used bioinformatic tools available for use, some of which I already have some basic familiarity with. For example, I have used SPAdes in the past to assemble phage genomes from short reads, and phage specific annotation tools like Phold and Pharokka to annotate the assemblies generated. Most directly applicable to my proposed work here, I have used breseq to call variants in bacterial genomes against a reference in the past. As such, this will be an application and combination of methods I have a basic understanding of in a more robust, in-depth, and reproducible manner, helping me to develop the core bioinformatics skill of documentation in addition to the direct use of bash and R. Finally, the data necessary for these analyses is already in hand in the Whiteson lab, meaning there is no concern about finding appropriate data or delays in generating it.
